<h1 class="da-title">Who pays for the AI boom?</h1>
<p class="da-lede">In 2026 four American cloud companies will spend about $740 billion on data centres, chips and power, roughly twice what they spent in 2025. They cannot pay for it out of cash alone any more, so they sell bonds, including a 100-year bond, move projects onto partners' balance sheets and lean on lenders who charge 7, 9 or 14 per cent. This is a guided tour of that money: who gives how much to whom, who lends on which terms, what interest rate each borrower pays, and what the bond market says about whether this is a bubble.</p>

**The question.** Every boom needs someone to write the cheques. In the first phase of this one the cheques came from the companies' own profits and from venture investors. In the second they came from a few large equity rounds. In 2026 they increasingly come from the bond market and from private credit funds, and that changes who is exposed: a bond is a promise to pay on a date, and a promise can be broken. So the useful questions are plain ones. Who has promised what to whom? At what interest rate? And what is the market charging today for the riskiest of those promises?

The article has five parts, and every figure in it can be played with.

1. **The bill.** How much is being spent and the four ways to pay for it.
2. **The web.** The AI labs, who invested in them, and the contracts that send the money straight back to the investors.
3. **The bonds.** How the big clouds borrow, how they borrow without appearing to, the ladder of interest rates from the safest to the riskiest borrower, who lends, and what the market charges today.
4. **The bubble question.** Four tests: can the revenue ever cover the capital charge, how long do the chips last, how does this boom compare in age and size with 1995 to 2000, and where would a break start.
5. **The same market.** Government bonds across twelve countries, because the AI borrowers sell their paper into the same market as every state.

<p class="da-part">Part I<strong>The bill</strong></p>

<h2 id="bill"><span class="num">01</span>The size of the bill</h2>

Start with the number everything else hangs on. After the July earnings season the four biggest US cloud companies (Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta) guided to **$735 to 750 billion of capital expenditure in calendar 2026**, up from "$640 billion and more" in January. In the second quarter alone they spent about $172 billion, 77 per cent more than a year earlier, and the first half of the year came to just under $305 billion {% include ai-ref.html k="platformonomics" %}. For scale, press tallies put the same four at roughly $410 billion for the whole of 2025 {% include ai-ref.html k="yahoo-capex" %}. Moody's, which also counts Oracle and CoreWeave, expects the six to spend about $785 billion in 2026 and to approach $1 trillion in 2027 {% include ai-ref.html k="moodys" %}.

{% include ai.html kind="capex" title="Capital expenditure of the four big clouds" caption="Capex including finance leases, calendar 2026 guidance at each quarter's results (midpoints of ranges) and the second quarter itself. Microsoft's July number is lower than April's because $15 billion was reclassified from finance leases to operating leases, not because it spends less. Platformonomics, 31 July 2026." %}

Two things in that figure are worth a second look. The guidance only moves one way, up, except for Microsoft's accounting change: Alphabet raised its range in April and again in July, Meta raised in April, Amazon went from $200 billion to $220 billion in July. And the quarter is not a plateau: Meta's capex in Q2 was 57 per cent higher than in Q1 {% include ai-ref.html k="platformonomics" %}.

What does the money buy? Mostly three things. **Chips**, of which Nvidia's data-centre business alone sold $89.0 billion in the quarter to July, up 117 per cent in a year {% include ai-ref.html k="nvidia-8k" %}. **Buildings and power**: shells, cooling, substations, the land. And **leases**, because a growing part of the capacity is rented from partners rather than owned. The first of these is the reason Nvidia's net income in a single quarter was $59.7 billion on revenue of $96.2 billion, with a gross margin of 75.0 per cent {% include ai-ref.html k="nvidia-8k" %}. Keep that figure in mind for the section on the dot-com boom: in this cycle the company that sells the shovels is making money on a scale that the shovel sellers of 2000 never did.

{: .da-bridge}
The bill is large and still growing. The next question is where it is paid from, because the answer decides who carries the risk.

<h2 id="four"><span class="num">02</span>Four ways to pay</h2>

There are four sources of money, and the trend is a slide from the first towards the other three.

**1. Cash the business earns.** For a decade the clouds paid for capex out of operating cash flow. That is running out. Alphabet's free cash flow was negative $5.9 billion in the second quarter, which one tally describes as the first negative quarter since the company listed {% include ai-ref.html k="aiweekly-cds" %} {% include ai-ref.html k="platformonomics" %}. Amazon's trailing free cash flow fell from $25.9 billion to $1.2 billion as equipment purchases rose by $59.3 billion {% include ai-ref.html k="ig-q2" %}. Meta's was $0.78 billion in the quarter {% include ai-ref.html k="platformonomics" %}. Microsoft says it expects to stay free-cash-flow positive in its fiscal 2027 {% include ai-ref.html k="platformonomics" %}. Trailing free cash flow of the four together is down 24 per cent on a year ago {% include ai-ref.html k="platformonomics" %}.

**2. Bonds sold in the company's own name.** The five biggest issuers (Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle) sold about $121 billion of US corporate bonds in 2025, against an average of $28 billion a year in 2020 to 2024 {% include ai-ref.html k="investing-supply" %}. In 2026 the pace is higher: one tally counts about $159 billion by early June {% include ai-ref.html k="itiger-supply" %}, another about $200 billion for hyperscalers by the autumn within roughly $500 billion of AI-related debt {% include ai-ref.html k="blackflower" %}, and Morgan Stanley expects about $570 billion of AI-related debt worldwide by year end, double the previous year {% include ai-ref.html k="startupfortune" %}. The figures differ because the definitions differ (which companies, which currencies, whether loans and securitisations count); the direction does not. Section 6 goes through the deals.

**3. Debt that sits with a partner.** A data-centre campus can be owned by a joint venture, financed by bonds that the joint venture sells, and leased to the cloud company, so that the debt does not appear as the cloud company's own. Meta's Hyperion campus in Louisiana is the largest example, with $27.3 billion of bonds; Section 7 explains how it works and what the bonds are worth now.

**4. Equity, and money from the sellers.** The AI labs, which do not earn enough to buy their own compute, raise equity from investors: $122 billion for OpenAI in the spring, $65 billion for Anthropic in May. Some of the investors are the very companies that sell the labs their compute and chips. And Nvidia, in its August results, announced "compute financing partnerships with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to mobilize over $500 billion of third-party capital" {% include ai-ref.html k="nvidia-8k" %}. When the chip maker arranges the financing for its own customers, that is a fifth source hiding inside the fourth, and it is the one with the longest history of ending badly, as Section 4 will show.

The mix matters because the four sources have very different failure modes. If cash flow disappoints, a company cuts capex and its shares fall. If a bond cannot be repaid, creditors take losses, ratings fall and the next borrower pays more. If a joint venture's tenant walks away, the loss lands on whoever holds the bonds, and the guarantee of the parent is limited and written in a footnote.

{: .da-bridge}
Before the bonds, the borrowers at the other end of the chain: the labs whose demand the whole build-out is a bet on.

<p class="da-part">Part II<strong>The web</strong></p>

<h2 id="labs"><span class="num">03</span>The labs: three companies that lose money at scale</h2>

Three companies train the models that the data centres exist to serve and run, and none of them is profitable by the numbers published so far.

**OpenAI** closed its latest round on 31 March 2026: $122 billion of committed capital at a post-money valuation of $852 billion. The round, announced in February at $110 billion, was anchored by Amazon (up to $50 billion, of which $35 billion is conditional on milestones), Nvidia ($30 billion) and SoftBank ($30 billion), with Andreessen Horowitz, D. E. Shaw and $3 billion from individual investors {% include ai-ref.html k="cnbc-openai" %}. On the revenue side, OpenAI reportedly took in $5.7 billion in the first quarter {% include ai-ref.html k="aiweekly-openai-q1" %} and $6.7 billion in the second {% include ai-ref.html k="cryptobriefing" %}. A presentation reported by the Financial Times in September forecasts revenue of $36 billion in 2026 and $350 billion in 2030, compute spending of about $856 billion through 2030, and **negative free cash flow of $278 billion from 2026 to 2030**; the same reports say OpenAI is discussing a new round at a valuation above $1.2 trillion {% include ai-ref.html k="lds-openai" %}.

**Anthropic** raised a $65 billion Series H at a $965 billion post-money valuation in May 2026, led by Altimeter, Dragoneer, Greenoaks and Sequoia {% include ai-ref.html k="simon-anthropic" %} {% include ai-ref.html k="lds-anthropic" %}, which made it, for the first time, worth more than OpenAI. Its annualised revenue was about $9 billion at the end of 2025 and over $30 billion in April, which Anthropic itself reported when it announced a multi-gigawatt TPU deal with Google and Broadcom {% include ai-ref.html k="anthropic-broadcom" %}. It then reached $47 billion in May and, according to Bloomberg's report of 17 August, topped **$65 billion at the end of July** {% include ai-ref.html k="runtimewire-anthropic" %}. A run rate takes a recent short period and multiplies it up; it is not booked annual revenue and Anthropic publishes no audited figures.

**xAI**, Elon Musk's lab, no longer exists as a separate company: SpaceX acquired it in February 2026 in a deal that valued xAI at about $250 billion and the combination at $1.25 trillion, and SpaceX listed on Nasdaq on 12 June, selling shares at $135 to raise about $75 billion at a valuation near $1.8 trillion {% include ai-ref.html k="spacex-ipo" %}. The prospectus showed xAI's numbers for the first time: revenue of $3.2 billion in 2025 against an operating loss of $6.36 billion and capex of $12.7 billion, then in the first quarter of 2026 revenue of $818 million, an operating loss of $2.47 billion and capex of $7.7 billion; in March SpaceX took a $20 billion bridge loan to refinance xAI's debt onto its own balance sheet {% include ai-ref.html k="yahoo-xai" %}. The same filing disclosed that Anthropic, a competitor, pays SpaceX **$1.25 billion a month** for compute in the Colossus data centres, about $15 billion a year through May 2029, terminable on 90 days' notice {% include ai-ref.html k="hothardware-spacex" %}. Rivals renting each other's computers is how tight the supply of capacity is.

{% include ai.html kind="labs" title="Three labs: valuation, revenue and what the market pays per dollar of revenue" caption="Valuations are post-money at the round or merger named in the tooltip. The revenue bases differ (OpenAI: a 2026 forecast; Anthropic: a run rate at the end of July; xAI: one quarter times four), so the ratios are indications, not like-for-like multiples." %}

Read the ratios with care. At face value the market pays about 24 times OpenAI's forecast 2026 revenue and 15 times Anthropic's current run rate. Those multiples are high but they are not absurd for companies whose revenue roughly doubles every few months; they are absurd if growth stops. The valuation is a bet on the growth rate more than on the level.

Three patterns are visible once you click through.

**The loop.** Amazon agreed to invest up to $50 billion in OpenAI, and OpenAI expanded its Amazon Web Services contract by $100 billion over eight years, on top of $38 billion agreed in 2025 {% include ai-ref.html k="cnbc-openai" %}. Amazon invested $8 billion in Anthropic and up to $25 billion more, and Anthropic committed to spend more than $100 billion on AWS through 2036 {% include ai-ref.html k="decrypt-amzn-anth" %}. Google committed up to $40 billion to Anthropic ($10 billion up front, $30 billion if targets are met) {% include ai-ref.html k="multiples-anthropic" %}, which in turn is buying about 1 gigawatt of Google TPUs this year and about 3.5 gigawatts more from 2027 {% include ai-ref.html k="anthropic-broadcom" %}. Microsoft holds about 27 per cent of OpenAI, valued at $135 billion in the October 2025 recapitalisation, and OpenAI agreed to buy an incremental $250 billion of Azure services {% include ai-ref.html k="msft-openai" %}. In each case the investor books an equity stake and a cloud customer at the same time. That is not improper (a supplier investing in a customer is common), but it means that revenue growth at the clouds and the valuations of the labs are partly the same money counted twice, once as an investment and once as a sale.

**The promise that wasn't.** In September 2025 Nvidia and OpenAI announced a letter of intent for Nvidia to invest up to $100 billion as OpenAI deployed at least 10 gigawatts of Nvidia systems {% include ai-ref.html k="bnn-nvidia" %}. In 2026 the Wall Street Journal reported that the plan had stalled; Jensen Huang said the $100 billion "was never a commitment", and Nvidia's actual investment in the spring round was $30 billion, which Huang indicated could be its last in OpenAI before a listing {% include ai-ref.html k="bs-huang" %} {% include ai-ref.html k="the-decoder" %}. The lesson for reading this graph: a headline number from a letter of intent is not a cash flow.

**The suppliers pay too.** AMD gave OpenAI warrants for up to 160 million AMD shares, about 10 per cent of the company, at one cent each, vesting as OpenAI deploys 6 gigawatts of AMD chips and as the AMD share price reaches milestones up to $600 {% include ai-ref.html k="xpu-amd" %}. Nvidia owns equity in CoreWeave and agreed to buy CoreWeave's unsold capacity through 2032 for up to $6.3 billion {% include ai-ref.html k="kavout-crwv" %}; it has put money into xAI, Nebius, IREN and others, over $40 billion across 2025 and 2026 by one count {% include ai-ref.html k="the-decoder" %}. The Decoder's summary of the pattern is the sceptic's one: "Others see a pattern of circular investments in these deals, particularly with so-called neoclouds, essentially pre-financing the sale of Nvidia's own GPUs" {% include ai-ref.html k="the-decoder" %}.

There is a precedent, and it is an uncomfortable one. In the telecom boom around 2000 the equipment makers financed their customers: Lucent committed $8.1 billion of customer financing, Nortel $3.1 billion and Cisco $2.4 billion, with vendor financing peaking at $25 to 30 billion in 2000 {% include ai-ref.html k="tunguz-nortel" %}. Of 37 listed US telecom service providers founded since 1996, 18 were bankrupt by the time the dust had settled, and Lucent's revenue fell 69 per cent from $37.9 billion in 1999 to $11.8 billion in 2002 {% include ai-ref.html k="tunguz-nortel" %} {% include ai-ref.html k="newsweek-loans" %}. The analogy has limits that Section 13 returns to, but it is why economists read circular financing as a warning sign rather than as proof of demand.

<p class="da-part">Part III<strong>The bonds</strong></p>

<h2 id="bonds"><span class="num">06</span>The clouds' bonds</h2>

A bond is a loan split into tradable pieces. The borrower sells pieces ("tranches") of different lengths, say 5 years, 10 years and 40 years, and promises each holder a fixed interest rate (the coupon) twice a year and the face value at the end. The price of the tranche is quoted as a **spread** over the US Treasury bond of similar length: if the Treasury yields 4 per cent and the company pays a 0.8 point spread, it pays 4.8 per cent. The spread is the market's price for the risk that this company, unlike the US government, does not pay. A spread of 0.01 percentage points is one basis point (bp).

The big clouds are rated AA or better (Microsoft is the one AAA; section 07 explains how the ratings are kept), one of the highest ratings in corporate lending (Amazon, for one, "continue[s] to maintain AA ratings" {% include ai-ref.html k="sageadvisory" %}), and until 2025 they hardly needed the bond market. Since then they have used it on a scale that is new:

- **Meta, 30 October 2025:** $30 billion in six tranches from 5 to 40 years, at spreads from +50 bp for the 5-year to +110 bp for the 40-year and coupons from 4.200 per cent (2030) to 5.750 per cent (2065); orders were about $125 billion, a record for the time {% include ai-ref.html k="ifre-meta" %}.
- **Oracle, 2 February 2026:** $25 billion in eight tranches from 3 to 40 years, the first part of a plan to raise up to $50 billion in debt and equity; the order book of more than $125 billion was the largest ever for a corporate bond, and the bonds came with concessions to Oracle's existing curve {% include ai-ref.html k="ifre-oracle" %}. Oracle also said it did not expect to issue further bonds in calendar 2026 {% include ai-ref.html k="oracle-q3" %}.
- **Alphabet, 9 and 10 February 2026:** $20 billion in seven dollar tranches (3.700 per cent 2029 to 5.750 per cent 2066, +27 to +95 bp, orders above $100 billion) {% include ai-ref.html k="streetinsider-alphabet" %}, then sterling and Swiss-franc bonds including a **£1 billion 100-year bond at 6.125 per cent**, the first century bond from a technology company since Motorola in 1997, for which £9.5 billion of bids were placed {% include ai-ref.html k="techloy-alphabet" %}.
- **Amazon, March and July 2026:** $37 billion in March {% include ai-ref.html k="investing-amzn" %}, a €14.5 billion euro deal in eight tranches {% include ai-ref.html k="blackflower" %}, and $25 billion in eight tranches on 7 July, from +40 bp (3-year) to +125 bp (40-year). Orders peaked at $62 billion and were later pared to about $41 billion, "cooler" than the March sale {% include ai-ref.html k="investing-amzn" %}. The deal pushed Amazon's existing 30-year bond about 20 bp wider {% include ai-ref.html k="sageadvisory" %}.
- **Meta, 30 April 2026:** $25 billion in six tranches, priced wider than the October deal almost across the curve, with orders of $96 billion at the peak against $125 billion {% include ai-ref.html k="bgov-meta" %}.

{% include ai.html kind="curves" title="What each tranche cost: the spread curves of three jumbo deals" caption="New-issue spread over the matching US Treasury, by time to maturity at issue. Switch to coupons to see the interest rate itself. Sources: IFR on Meta's October 2025 deal, StreetInsider on Alphabet, Investing.com on Amazon." %}

Three things stand out. First, **the curve steepens with the length of the loan**, as it always does, but the long end is where the pressure shows: the same 40-year paper cost +110 bp for Meta in October 2025, +95 bp for Alphabet in February and +125 bp for Amazon in July. Second, **demand is fading at the margin even though it is still strong**. Reports put the order-book cover (orders divided by the amount sold) at about 5 times in February and about 2 times in July {% include ai-ref.html k="startupfortune" %}. New-issue concessions, the discount a borrower offers to get a deal done, ran around 12 bp for the AI-related deals one analysis tracks, against about 2.5 bp for the bond market at large {% include ai-ref.html k="investing-supply" %}. Third, **the supply is large against the market that absorbs it**: technology now makes up about 18 per cent of US investment-grade supply, double its 2025 share and a record {% include ai-ref.html k="startupfortune" %}, and the spreads of the group have widened: Amazon, Microsoft, Meta and Alphabet traded about 80 bp over Treasuries in September, up from about 50 bp {% include ai-ref.html k="startupfortune" %}.

A sanity check on the numbers, which I can do because the Treasury yields are public: on 9 February the 30-year Treasury yielded 4.85 per cent, and Alphabet's 2066 bond (priced against the 30-year) came with a 5.75 per cent coupon, a spread of 0.90 to 0.95, in line with the +95 bp reported. For Meta on 30 October 2025, 4.65 per cent plus +110 bp gives 5.75 per cent, the coupon of the 2065 bond. For Amazon on 7 July the 30-year yielded 5.05 per cent, so the +125 bp 40-year was priced at about 6.30 per cent, which is the figure I use in the ladder below.

<h2 id="spv"><span class="num">07</span>Borrowing without borrowing</h2>

The best-known structure is **Hyperion**. In October 2025 Meta and the asset manager Blue Owl formed a joint venture to build a data-centre campus in Louisiana that Mark Zuckerberg said would be the size of midtown and lower Manhattan. Blue Owl funds put in equity of roughly $2.4 to $3 billion (reports differ) for 80 per cent of the venture, and Meta kept 20 per cent {% include ai-ref.html k="marcellus" %} {% include ai-ref.html k="ifre-beignet" %}. The venture's financing vehicle, **Beignet Investor LLC**, sold **$27.3 billion of 6.581 per cent secured notes due 2049**, rated A+, one notch below Meta's own AA-: the biggest single-tranche corporate bond on record {% include ai-ref.html k="ifre-beignet" %} {% include ai-ref.html k="marcellus" %}. Pimco bought about $18 billion and BlackRock more than $3 billion {% include ai-ref.html k="marcellus" %}. Meta leases the campus from the venture and gave it a **residual value guarantee** covering the first 16 years: if at the end of the lease the campus is worth less than expected, Meta pays the difference, up to a cap. The campus and the debt stay off Meta's balance sheet; the guarantee is described in the notes to the accounts {% include ai-ref.html k="aol-hyperion" %}. A line from Bloomberg's coverage of the deal sums up the product:

<blockquote class="ai-q" markdown="0"><p>"It's a fixed-income risk with an equity-like return."</p><cite>Quoted in Bloomberg's "Short read: three AI megadeals", as reproduced by Marcellus</cite></blockquote>

The structure is not improper and not secret; it is the same lease-financing logic as for an airline's aircraft. What it does is move the price of risk. Meta's own bonds were sold with coupons of 4.2 to 5.75 per cent depending on length (4.875 per cent for ten years); the Hyperion notes paid 6.581 per cent for 23.6 years because lenders get Meta's rent and a limited guarantee, not Meta's full promise. That extra yield is the price of the difference.

And the price has moved. As of Monday 19 October the Beignet bonds trade at about **91 cents on the dollar, a yield of 7.55 per cent and a spread of 230 bp over Treasuries**, against 185 bp at issue and a peak of 255 bp in July {% include ai-ref.html k="edge-oct19" %}. On 23 September they stood at 94.4, a record low then and 14 per cent below the roughly 110 they had reached at their peak; on a mark-to-market basis Pimco's $18 billion position was down by roughly $1 billion {% include ai-ref.html k="hakky" %}. Meta's default swaps, meanwhile, are near 100 bp, above the July peak {% include ai-ref.html k="edge-oct19" %}. A bond at 91 does not mean Meta will fail to pay the rent; it means holders now demand 7.55 per cent to carry 23-year risk of a campus whose rent is guaranteed for 16, in a world where the 30-year Treasury yields 5.4 per cent.

**How such a vehicle is built.** Three layers, each a newly formed company with one purpose. At the bottom a joint-venture company owns the land and the buildings: Blue Owl funds hold 80 per cent, Meta 20 per cent. Above it sits Beignet Investor, which sells the notes and passes the money down; the notes are secured on the venture's assets and on the lease {% include ai-ref.html k="ifre-gymnastics" %}. Meta is the tenant. Its lease runs in **four-year non-cancellable blocks with options that can take total occupancy to about 20 years**, and on top of that sits the residual value guarantee, which covers 16 years, with a termination fee for the last four {% include ai-ref.html k="ifre-gymnastics" %}. The standard design of such vehicles (a charter that limits the company to this one business, independent directors, no mixing of its money with the sponsor's) makes them "bankruptcy-remote": if Meta got into trouble, its other creditors could not reach the campus, and the noteholders could not claim Meta's other assets beyond the lease and the guarantee. That last point is how these vehicles are usually built, not something I read in the Hyperion documents, which I have not seen.

**The laws it works around, one by one.** Nothing here is hidden; each step uses a rule exactly as it is written.

| Rule | What it asks | How Hyperion answers it | Who disagrees |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Consolidation** (US GAAP, the "variable interest entity" test written after Enron) | Does the sponsor have the *power* to direct the activities that matter most, *and* the exposure to the entity's profits and losses? If both, it must put the entity's debt on its own balance sheet | Meta: "As we do not have the power to direct the activities that most significantly impact the Venture's economic performance, we are not the primary beneficiary and, therefore, do not consolidate" {% include ai-ref.html k="meta-10q" %}. IFR: the structure is "designed so that ... the finger points at Blue Owl for consolidation, not Meta" {% include ai-ref.html k="ifre-gymnastics" %} | Meta's auditor EY called the judgment "especially challenging due to the significant judgment required" {% include ai-ref.html k="slashdot-ey" %} |
| **Lease accounting** (a lease is a finance lease, and therefore a debt, if it covers most of the asset's life or renewal is reasonably certain) | How long, and how sure | Short non-cancellable blocks with options: Meta can say renewal is not certain, so the lease stays off the balance sheet {% include ai-ref.html k="ifre-gymnastics" %} | Moody's: the reported "$662 billion" of data-centre leases signed but not yet started is 113 per cent of the five biggest spenders' adjusted debt {% include ai-ref.html k="fortune-662" %} |
| **Guarantees** (a loss is booked only if it is "probable") | How likely is a payout | The guarantee has a threshold of about $28 billion that falls over time; "RVG payments are not probable, and therefore no liability has been recorded" {% include ai-ref.html k="meta-10q" %}. The filing does show a maximum exposure of about $46 billion in total {% include ai-ref.html k="meta-10q" %} | S&P makes no debt adjustment "due to significant headroom between the RVG and a third-party appraisal value"; Moody's expects "a material increase in adjusted debt" {% include ai-ref.html k="ifre-moodys" %} |

The point of the table is that the three rules are *tests of form*. Whoever controls the venture, how long the lease is, how probable a payout is: each can be arranged, and the arrangement is legal. The IFR title puts it as "off-balance-sheet gymnastics 24 years after Enron" {% include ai-ref.html k="ifre-gymnastics" %}.

**The "boring limits".** For a company like Meta there are almost no hard limits of the textbook kind. Investment-grade bonds carry no maintenance financial covenants (no promise to keep debt below a multiple of earnings, which is what a leveraged loan has); the protection is a short list: a **limit on liens** (the "negative pledge": if the company pledges an asset to a new lender, it must secure the bondholders equally), restrictions on selling and leasing back assets, a merger covenant and a change-of-control put {% include ai-ref.html k="overbond-cov" %}. A vehicle neither owned nor controlled by the company is not the company's asset, so the pledge of the campus to Beignet's noteholders triggers no lien clause for Meta's own bondholders. I have not read Meta's indentures, so whether they have exceptions that matter here I cannot say. The limits that bite are the rating agencies' thresholds: each agency adds debt-like items back to reported debt and compares the sum with cash flow, and a company that crosses a ratio is downgraded and pays more on every later bond.

**The ratings, and a correction to a common phrase.** Only one of the four big clouds is triple-A. S&P rates **Microsoft AAA** (with Johnson & Johnson the only US public companies at that level) {% include ai-ref.html k="yahoo-aaa" %}; Alphabet was upgraded to **AA+** {% include ai-ref.html k="cityam-alphabet" %}, Amazon to **AA** from AA- {% include ai-ref.html k="cityam-alphabet" %} and Meta sits at **AA-**; Hyperion's notes are A+, "Meta minus one" {% include ai-ref.html k="ifre-gymnastics" %}. Two things keep the top ratings. The first is real: huge operating cash flow against the debt that is on the books. The second is the structure: Microsoft has issued almost no senior unsecured debt this year and pushes capacity into leases and project vehicles {% include ai-ref.html k="luminix" %}, so the obligation is a rent, not a bond. The two agencies differ in how much of that rent they count. S&P, on Hyperion, counts none of the guarantee; Moody's says it "will continue to assess cash exposures and debt-like adjustments" and may make "non-standard" ones {% include ai-ref.html k="fortune-662" %} {% include ai-ref.html k="ifre-moodys" %}. I found no statement in the sources I could read from either agency that Microsoft's own AAA is at risk, so whether the top rating survives the lease commitments is not something these sources decide.

**What does not change.** The campus is real, the rent is real and Meta's guarantee is real; only its place in the headline debt figure moves. The lenders know it, which is why the price moves: Hyperion's notes pay 6.581 per cent against 5.75 per cent for Meta's own 40-year bonds, and trade at a 7.55 per cent yield today. The risk that moved off Meta's balance sheet landed in Pimco's and BlackRock's portfolios.

The same template appears elsewhere.

- **QTS, Blackstone's data-centre arm,** sold bonds in April to refinance a campus in Georgia that Microsoft rents. The bonds were expected to be rated investment grade yet yield in the low to mid 7 per cent range, "similar to single-B junk debt" {% include ai-ref.html k="briefs-qts" %}, with $12.5 billion of peak demand {% include ai-ref.html k="bloomberg-qts" %}. Microsoft itself has issued almost no senior unsecured debt this year and pushes capacity into leases and project vehicles {% include ai-ref.html k="luminix" %}.
- **BlackRock's "Project Sopaipilla"** is raising more than $12 billion for Meta's El Paso data centre, described as one of the largest single infrastructure financings on record {% include ai-ref.html k="startupfortune" %}.
- **Abilene, Texas,** the first Stargate campus that serves OpenAI: a joint venture of Blue Owl, Crusoe and Primary Digital Infrastructure borrowed $2.3 billion from JPMorgan for the first phase and $7.1 billion for the second (1.2 gigawatts in all), and the campus is leased to Oracle {% include ai-ref.html k="commercialobserver" %}. Oracle then bought about $40 billion of Nvidia chips for it {% include ai-ref.html k="sdx-abilene" %}.
- **xAI** financed its GPUs through a special-purpose vehicle managed by Valor Equity Partners and through Apollo, which bought $3.5 billion of xAI debt at 99 cents on the dollar with a **10 per cent coupon** and about $7 billion in total {% include ai-ref.html k="techleap-apollo" %}. The SpaceX group is now reported to be seeking $40 billion of debt, about $10 billion in bank loans and $30 billion of investment-grade paper, led by Apollo, to buy Nvidia chips {% include ai-ref.html k="investinglive-spacex" %}.

{: .da-bridge}
Line these up by interest rate and you get a ladder, from the Treasury at the top to the riskiest borrower at the bottom.

Reading from the top. The **state** pays 5.1 per cent for ten years and 5.4 per cent for thirty. The **hyperscalers**, on their own names, pay 5.75 per cent for 40 years (Alphabet in February, Meta in October) and about 6.3 per cent for Amazon in July: a premium of about 1 point over the state for 40 years, but that premium is a spread, and it is wider now than it was in 2025. **Off-balance-sheet** structures pay 6.6 per cent at issue, 7.4 per cent in the Microsoft-linked QTS case and 7.55 per cent in the secondary market for Hyperion: roughly 2 to 2.5 points over the state. At the bottom, **the AI clouds and the labs' sponsors** pay 9 to 10 per cent or more: SoftBank (rated BB+, which is junk) paid 8.625 per cent for 3.5 years and 9.75 per cent for 7.5 years in September, CoreWeave sold 9.25 per cent notes due 2030 in 2025 {% include ai-ref.html k="fenwick-crwv" %} and $1.75 billion of 9.75 per cent notes due 2031 at par in April {% include ai-ref.html k="coreweave-pr" %}; the yield on its 2031 bond rose to 16 per cent this week and settled near 14 per cent {% include ai-ref.html k="investing-cds" %}, a level at which the market is saying that a default is a real possibility; xAI's debt to Apollo carried a 10 per cent coupon.

CoreWeave is the instructive case. In August it closed a $2.6 billion delayed-draw term loan at **SOFR plus 5.5 points** (about 9.4 per cent today), maturing in 2031. It had been marketed at SOFR plus 4.25 to 4.5 points and 99 cents; it closed at 97 cents after investors pushed back. The loan is secured on the assets of the borrowing unit and on take-or-pay customer contracts, reportedly with Anthropic, Jane Street and Hudson River Trading, whose average term is about three years: a five-year loan against three-year contracts. Total debt principal was $25.15 billion at the end of March, against $2.24 billion of unrestricted cash and first-quarter revenue of $2.08 billion {% include ai-ref.html k="energymag-crwv" %}. Its five-year default swap trades above 1,000 bp {% include ai-ref.html k="investing-cds" %}. With the usual simple assumption of a 40 per cent recovery that is a hazard of about 18 per cent a year, or a default probability of roughly 60 per cent over five years (write-ups of the same swap put it at 63 to 64 per cent {% include ai-ref.html k="biggo-crwv" %}). "CoreWeave is quickly becoming the AI trade's canary in the coal mine", in the words of XTB's research director Kathleen Brooks {% include ai-ref.html k="investing-cds" %}.

{: .da-bridge}
Someone has to hold all this paper. Who it is says a lot about where the losses would land.

<h2 id="lenders"><span class="num">09</span>Who lends</h2>

**Investment-grade bond funds and insurers.** The AA bonds of the clouds are bought by the same buyers that hold the rest of the investment-grade market: asset managers, pension funds and insurers. They buy them partly because the indices force them to. Technology is about 10 per cent of the Bloomberg corporate bond index {% include ai-ref.html k="investing-supply" %}; AI-related bonds outstanding amount to about $600 billion, roughly 6 per cent of the entire US investment-grade market, from 31 issuers of which five account for more than half; KKR's estimate is that the share could reach 20 per cent {% include ai-ref.html k="blackflower" %}. That is the sense in which the bond market inherits the concentration of the stock market: the same few names, priced in spreads instead of multiples {% include ai-ref.html k="blackflower" %}.

**Pimco and BlackRock** are the large single buyers of the structured deals: $18 billion and more than $3 billion of Hyperion, respectively {% include ai-ref.html k="marcellus" %}. **Apollo** is the lender of choice for the lower rungs: xAI's GPU debt, the proposed $40 billion for SpaceX, and Nvidia's financing partnership {% include ai-ref.html k="techleap-apollo" %} {% include ai-ref.html k="investinglive-spacex" %} {% include ai-ref.html k="nvidia-8k" %}.

**Private credit funds** lend to data-centre projects directly. They raised their money from wealthy individuals and institutions on the promise of quarterly liquidity, and this year the promise was tested. In the first quarter investors asked to withdraw 40.7 per cent of the shares in Blue Owl's $6.2 billion technology-income fund and 21.9 per cent of its $36 billion credit-income fund, $5.4 billion in all; Blue Owl met 5 per cent of the requests. Redemption requests above 5 per cent of shares were also reported at funds run by Apollo, Blackstone, BlackRock and Cliffwater, and UBS analysts sketched a scenario in which private-credit defaults reach about 15 per cent {% include ai-ref.html k="bisnow" %}. The immediate trigger was the fear that AI would hollow out software companies, which private credit lent to heavily, rather than data-centre loans; but Blue Owl is also a large lender to data-centre projects, and it is Hyperion's sponsor.

**Securitisation** is the other channel. Data-centre bonds backed by lease payments (ABS) and by mortgages on the buildings (CMBS) are growing from a small base: digital-infrastructure ABS issuance reached $24.3 billion by the end of May and was on pace to exceed $50 billion for the year for the first time {% include ai-ref.html k="firsteagle" %}; combined data-centre ABS and CMBS issuance was $26 billion in 2025, more than ten times 2020 {% include ai-ref.html k="impax" %}; US private-label CMBS issuance passed $76 billion in the first seven months of 2026, of which data centres were 9.8 per cent of the single-asset deals {% include ai-ref.html k="scotsman" %}.

**The banks** supply the construction loans that are later refinanced by bonds, such as JPMorgan's loans for Abilene, and a $40 billion loan facility to SoftBank signed in March, its largest dollar loan ever, partly to fund its follow-on investment in OpenAI {% include ai-ref.html k="tnw-softbank" %}. SoftBank is a good example of how the chain loops back on itself: it owns about 13 per cent of OpenAI after committing $64.6 billion; it borrowed against its Arm shares (a $25 billion margin loan) and against its OpenAI shares (it has sought a $10 billion margin loan at roughly SOFR plus 425 bp); and in September it sold $11.1 billion of bonds at 7.125 to 9.75 per cent to fund the October instalment to OpenAI. S&P puts its loan-to-value at 33 per cent against SoftBank's own 13 per cent calculation {% include ai-ref.html k="tnw-softbank" %} {% include ai-ref.html k="thepaypers-softbank" %}.

What this section cannot tell you, because nobody has published it, is the final holder of most of the structured paper, and how much of it sits with leveraged vehicles. Section 14 comes back to that.

So much for who holds the debt. What does it cost this week, and what are the markets saying about it?

The backdrop is a rising-rate year. The Federal Reserve raised its policy rate by 25 bp in September to 3.75 to 4.00 per cent, with a 12 to 0 vote, and 16 of 18 officials projected at least one more hike by year end; the Fed's 2026 forecast for PCE inflation was 3.7 per cent {% include ai-ref.html k="atfx" %}. The ten-year Treasury yield, 4.05 per cent a year ago, reached 5.31 per cent on 5 October, and the thirty-year reached 5.67 per cent two days later, a level not seen since 2002 {% include ai-ref.html k="wealthprofessional" %}. On 15 October, the last day in the data here, they stood at 5.10 and 5.43 per cent.

A default swap is insurance against a company missing payments: the number is the yearly premium in basis points on the insured amount, so 288 bp means $2.88 million a year to insure $100 million of Oracle debt for five years. A premium of 100 bp is what the market asks for a solidly investment-grade borrower with some stress; 1,000 bp is the price of a borrower the market thinks is more likely than not to default within the term. At the end of the first half the total net amount of default swaps outstanding on the major tech names was a record $12.5 billion, five times the second quarter of 2025, and Oracle alone made up $6.5 billion of that {% include ai-ref.html k="aiweekly-cds" %}.

Oracle is the large company at the centre. It was downgraded by S&P to BBB-, one notch above junk, with a negative outlook at S&P and Moody's; it ended its fiscal year with debt of about $130 billion and free cash flow of negative $23.7 billion after $55.7 billion on data centres {% include ai-ref.html k="aiweekly-cds" %}; and it has been sued by bondholders who say it concealed how much debt it would have to sell to support the $300 billion OpenAI contract {% include ai-ref.html k="dcd-oracle" %}. The company's own line is that most of the equipment for the big AI contracts is "either funded upfront via customer prepayments" or bought by the customer and supplied to Oracle, so that it does not expect to raise further funds for them {% include ai-ref.html k="oracle-q3" %}. The bond market has not been persuaded.

Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid put the change in one sentence on the day of the records:

<blockquote class="ai-q" markdown="0"><p>"This is not the first time we've had a sizeable market wobble on the back of AI fears, but as we move to a capex cycle funded out of debt rather than simply free cash flow, we have to increasingly respect the credit price action."</p><cite>Jim Reid, Deutsche Bank, quoted by Reuters, 15 October 2026</cite></blockquote>

The reasoning behind the quote is the point of this whole article: a stock price can be wrong for years without consequences for the company, because shareholders have no date on which they must be paid. A bond has such a date.

That is the market's verdict so far. Four tests follow that ask whether it is a fair one, starting with the arithmetic of the break-even.

<p class="da-part">Part IV<strong>The bubble question</strong></p>

<h2 id="breakeven"><span class="num">11</span>Test 1: the break-even</h2>

Suppose a company spends $C$ on data centres in one year. It must earn back the money borrowed or raised, plus the return its investors require, before the equipment wears out in $L$ years. If the required return is $r$, the yearly cost of capital on that spending is the standard annuity payment

$$
a(r, L) \;=\; \frac{r}{1 - (1 + r)^{-L}}
$$

per dollar, and the revenue $R$ needed each year, if the gross margin of the service is $m$, is

$$
R \;=\; \frac{C \cdot a(r, L)}{m}.
$$

With $C = \$750$ billion (about the four clouds' 2026 capex), $L = 6$ years, $r = 8\ \%$ and $m = 60\ \%$, the annuity factor is 0.216, the capital charge is **$162 billion a year** and the revenue needed is **$270 billion a year, for each year's vintage of spending**. If the spending is repeated every year for six years, six vintages overlap and the charge becomes $973 billion a year, which needs **$1.6 trillion** of revenue at a 60 per cent margin. Move the sliders to see how sensitive this is: a three-year life instead of six nearly doubles the need.

{% include ai.html kind="calc" title="How much revenue does a year of AI capex have to earn?" caption="A simple capital-charge calculation, not a forecast: it ignores growth in the capacity's usefulness, residual value, taxes and power costs, and it treats all capex as AI capex. The comparison bars are what the sources report today; cloud revenue includes non-AI work." %}

The outside estimates are of the same order as the repeated-spending case. Bain's annual technology report estimated that by 2030 AI companies would need **$2 trillion** a year in combined revenue to fund the compute demand and would fall about **$800 billion short** {% include ai-ref.html k="bain" %}. Sequoia's David Cahn put 2026 AI infrastructure spending at $1.5 trillion and the revenue needed to justify it at roughly **$3 trillion** {% include ai-ref.html k="cahn" %}. Against that, current AI-attributable revenue is somewhere between $50 and $150 billion by one estimate {% include ai-ref.html k="luminix-gap" %}, and the revenue the labs report is $36 billion (OpenAI, 2026 forecast) and $65 billion (Anthropic's run rate).

The counter-evidence is also real, and it is the reason this is a debate and not a verdict.

- **The clouds are growing faster, not slower.** In the second quarter Google Cloud revenue grew 82 per cent to $24.8 billion with operating margin up from 20.7 to 35.6 per cent, AWS grew 37 per cent to $42.2 billion (its fastest in 18 quarters), and Azure grew 43 per cent at constant currency and passed $100 billion annualised {% include ai-ref.html k="upperedge" %}. Moody's counted median revenue growth of the hyperscalers accelerating from 26 per cent at the end of 2023 to 39 per cent at the end of 2025 {% include ai-ref.html k="moodys" %}.
- **Demand is contracted.** Backlogs of $514 billion at Google Cloud {% include ai-ref.html k="upperedge" %}, $553 billion at Oracle {% include ai-ref.html k="oracle-q3" %} and $99.4 billion at CoreWeave {% include ai-ref.html k="energymag-crwv" %} are signed, though much of it comes from the labs, whose ability to pay is what is in question.
- **The labs' revenue is compounding.** Anthropic went from about $9 billion to $65 billion of run rate in seven months; at that pace the gap in the calculator closes quickly. The gap is not a gap in demand but one in time: the capex comes first, the revenue later.
- **The chip seller is already profitable.** Nvidia earned $59.7 billion in one quarter {% include ai-ref.html k="nvidia-8k" %}.

Moody's, which rates many of these borrowers, summarised the dilemma in terms the quoted report reproduces: "Hyperscalers perceive underinvestment in AI as an existential threat, while some investors worry that aggressive spending could lead to overbuilding and weaker returns" {% include ai-ref.html k="moodys" %}. It also warned that higher capital intensity and debt could lead to a "reassessment of creditworthiness" if profit growth fails to materialise {% include ai-ref.html k="moodys" %}. Note the second sentence is about borrowers and it is conditional: the agencies, unlike equity analysts, are not saying that the spending is wrong, only that it has made the borrowers more fragile.

{: .da-bridge}
The calculator turns on one assumption above all: how long the equipment lasts. That is Test 2.

<h2 id="chips"><span class="num">12</span>Test 2: how long a chip lasts</h2>

A bond that matures in 2049 or 2066 is lent against assets that must still be worth something then. A building shell, a substation and a grid connection can plausibly last decades. A GPU cannot. The accounting depreciation the companies chose says what they believe: Alphabet and Microsoft depreciate servers over six years, Meta over 5.5 years (the lengthening alone lowered its depreciation by $2.3 billion in the first nine months of 2025), and Amazon moved to six years in 2024 and then back to five for some servers {% include ai-ref.html k="yahoo-depr" %}.

The sceptic is the investor Michael Burry, who argues that the economic life is two to three years and that the hyperscalers therefore understate depreciation by about $176 billion between 2026 and 2028, overstating Oracle's profit by roughly 27 per cent and Meta's by 21 per cent {% include ai-ref.html k="burry" %}. The defence, from Nvidia and CoreWeave, is that a GPU does not retire when a newer one ships: it moves from training the frontier model to serving inference to lighter jobs, and CoreWeave cites five-year customer contracts and resale values around 95 per cent for A100 and H100 chips {% include ai-ref.html k="burry" %} {% include ai-ref.html k="fourweek-depr" %}. Both claims come from interested parties. What can be said without taking sides is what the debt structure implies.

- Long bonds against campuses (Hyperion to 2049 with a 16-year guarantee) are bets on the building and on the tenant.
- Shorter secured loans against GPUs (CoreWeave's to 2031, xAI's Apollo debt) are bets on the chips *and* on the contracts that pay for them. CoreWeave's contracts average about three years against a five-year loan, so the loan outlives the contract that services it unless it is renewed on similar terms {% include ai-ref.html k="energymag-crwv" %}.
- The residual-value guarantee in Hyperion is the sharpest version of the question: it assumes someone will want a 16-year-old campus built for chips that will have been replaced five times.

If useful life is shorter than the books say, three things follow. Reported profits of the clouds are overstated (the accounting is a choice); the break-even of Test 1 moves out; and, most relevant for the lenders, collateral that is worth less than the loan leaves the lender dependent on the borrower's revenue instead of the asset. This is the usual reason private credit and bank lending to the neoclouds is priced as it is: 9 per cent and more, for an asset that rolls over faster than a loan.

{: .da-bridge}
So far the tests ask whether the cash flows add up. A different test asks whether the market looks like earlier ones.

<h2 id="dotcom"><span class="num">13</span>Test 3: boom against boom</h2>

The dot-com comparison is the reflex, so here it is with numbers. The figure rebases the Nasdaq Composite to 100 on the month a technology became mainstream: Netscape's listing in August 1995, ChatGPT's release in November 2022. Switch to the long-run view to see the full history on a log scale.

{% include ai.html kind="nasdaq" title="The Nasdaq in two booms" caption="Nasdaq Composite, month-end closes, FRED (NASDAQCOM). The anchoring dates are my choice; a different anchor shifts the picture. A marker shows where today falls and where the 2000 peak fell." %}

Three observations. **In age,** this boom is 47 months old by the Nasdaq's measure; the dot-com run peaked after 55 months, in March 2000, at 4.5 times its August 1995 level. At the same age (July 1999) the dot-com Nasdaq stood at 2.6 times its start; the current one stands at 2.3 times. In 2026 the index made its record close of 27,600 on 6 October, and on 15 October it was 3.2 per cent below that. **In size,** the dot-com index fell 78 per cent from its March 2000 peak to its October 2002 trough; that is the downside of the comparison, not a forecast. **In earnings,** Nvidia's quarterly net income of $59.7 billion has no equivalent in 1999; the dot-com leaders were mostly valued on prospects, and the main suppliers (Cisco, Lucent) earned a fraction of that.

Three differences argue that this is not a repeat, and three similarities that it rhymes.

*Different.* The profits of the main chip supplier are enormous and real. The buyers of the chips are, for the most part, among the most profitable companies in history. And the market multiple on the leader is moderate: Nvidia's forward price-to-earnings ratio is quoted between about 22 and 35 depending on the source and the day, against a wider market at about 21 {% include ai-ref.html k="fool-nvda" %}.

*Similar.* The customers of the dot-com equipment makers were start-ups, financed by the vendors; today's biggest customers of the chip supply chain are labs whose revenue is a fraction of their promised spending, financed partly by their vendors (Section 4). The leverage is moving from the equity of profitable firms to bonds and private credit (Section 10). And the official sector is worried: the Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee said in late 2025 that "the risk of a sharp market correction has increased", with stock valuations "comparable to the peak" of the dot-com era, and the IMF's managing director said that valuations were "heading toward levels we saw during the bullishness about the internet 25 years ago" {% include ai-ref.html k="boe-imf" %}.

The point that matters for the bond holder is that the dot-com crash was mainly an equity event for the shares of the internet companies, and a **debt** event for the telecom carriers that had financed fibre with bonds and vendor loans. This cycle has the first features and, increasingly, the second.

The ranking suggests a chain rather than a cliff. The bottom three rows borrow against contracts with a few counterparties: the labs. The labs' ability to pay depends on raising new equity every year (OpenAI's forecast says it needs one) and on revenue growth that has so far been faster than the financing needs. If the equity window narrowed, the first to feel it would be those whose bonds are repaid out of lab payments: the neoclouds, then Oracle, then the vehicles. The big four have enough cash flow to cut capex before missing a coupon, which is why their bonds are still priced as AA credits at 50 to 80 bp, though that spread is rising. The path that would hurt more is the one in which interest rates rise further while the labs' equity rounds slow, which is why the next Fed meetings matter to the AI story.

**What would change my mind**, in the order in which it would show up: whether OpenAI's rumoured round above $1.2 trillion is completed {% include ai-ref.html k="lds-openai" %}; the order-book cover and concessions on the next jumbo bond (cover below 2 and concessions above 12 bp would mean the primary market is closing); CoreWeave's ability to refinance below 10 per cent; the 2031 yield falling back below 10 per cent or Hyperion recovering to par; and the Fed's path, because 3.7 per cent inflation is the thing that keeps the Treasury yield above the level at which borrowing for 40 years looks cheap.

<p class="da-part">Part V<strong>The same market</strong></p>

<h2 id="sovereign"><span class="num">15</span>The same market: government bonds</h2>

The AI borrowers do not raise money in a separate market. They sell 10-, 30- and 40-year bonds to the same pension funds, insurers and asset managers that buy government bonds, and they quote their spreads over the government curve. When the government's yield rises, so do their costs, whatever their credit. And governments are themselves issuing record amounts: the US federal deficit is around $2 trillion, which is one of the reasons for the rise in the term premium {% include ai-ref.html k="kucoin-tp" %}.

So what has been happening to the price of government debt? The bond price moves opposite to its yield: a government bond that pays a fixed 1.5 per cent is worth less when new bonds pay 5 per cent. The figure below shows ten-year yields in twelve countries since 2019 and, below the chart, what a ten-year bond bought at par in the month you choose would be worth today, next to each country's interest bill and debt from the national-budgets article.

{% include ai.html kind="sov" title="Government bond yields in twelve countries, and what a bond bought earlier is worth now" caption="OECD long-term government bond yields (about ten years), monthly, via FRED, to September 2026 (August for Italy and the Netherlands). Interest and debt from the dataset behind the national-budgets article. The price calculation is a simplified one: annual coupons, no tax, no currency." %}

The table at the default date, December 2021, shows how violent the repricing has been. A ten-year bond bought at par in December 2021 has lost about **16 per cent of its price** for a US buyer, **20 per cent** for a German or British one, **20 per cent** for a French one and **14 per cent** for a Japanese one, because yields went from 1.5 to 5.0 per cent in the US, from −0.4 to 3.5 per cent in Germany, from 0.8 to 5.2 per cent in the UK and from 0.07 to 3.05 per cent in Japan. Not one of these is a default; it is the arithmetic of a coupon fixed in an era of near-zero rates. Slide the purchase date to 2020 to see the worst of it, or to early 2026 to see how much of the damage is recent.

For the budgets the other side of the same arithmetic is the interest bill. The [national-budgets article](/blog/2026/national-budgets/#interest) shows what governments pay on their debts as a share of GDP: 4.0 per cent in the United States and in Italy, 3.5 per cent in Canada, 3.0 per cent in the United Kingdom. As old, cheap debt matures and is replaced by new debt at 4 to 5 per cent, those percentages rise even if no one borrows another dollar. That is the second route by which the AI build-out and the budgets meet: if companies and the state compete for the same long-term savings, the price of that saving goes up for both, and the AI borrowers, sitting at the risky end of the ladder in Section 8, feel it first.

<h2 id="limits"><span class="num">16</span>What this article cannot tell you</h2>

- **Secondary sourcing.** Much of the reporting is from Bloomberg, Reuters, the FT and CNBC, paywalled or blocked for an automated reader. For most of the deals I relied on search snippets and on aggregators that quote them; the reference list marks each source as "opened" (I read the page) or "snippet" (I saw only what a search result printed). Numbers are therefore "as reported", and a single misprint upstream would be repeated here. I tried to use at least two sources for every number that matters; where there is only one, it is flagged.
- **Time stamps.** Bond prices, default swaps and rates change daily. Prices are stamped; the last Treasury and spread observations are of 15 October 2026 and Hyperion's price is of 19 October.
- **Not additive.** Most deal sizes in the network figure are "up to" amounts, multi-year contracts, letters of intent or stakes valued at a round; they cannot be summed into one "total investment in AI".
- **Definitions of AI debt differ.** The estimates of 2026 issuance ($200, $240, $450 or $570 billion) differ in the borrowers they include and the instruments they count. I give the range rather than choose one.
- **Who holds the risk.** Final holders of the structured paper, the leverage of the funds that bought it, and the banks' exposures to private-credit funds are not public in the sources I could read. This is the most important gap.
- **Not a forecast.** The calculator, the Nasdaq comparison and the table of layers are ways of looking, not predictions. A market can be in a bubble for years, and a boom financed by debt can also simply work.
- **Not advice.** Nothing here is investment, trading or policy advice.
